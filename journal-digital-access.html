When digital access becomes a barrier to care

Healthcare can move online faster than people gain the means to use it. A recent review offers a starting point for asking who is left out.

By Sasha Gladkikh
Founder and Executive Director, Beyond BordersSeptember 19, 2026
4 min read

An online appointment tells us what a service offers. It does not tell us whether someone can book it, understand the instructions, or attend. Evaluating digital healthcare means examining that whole journey.

Three ideas to take away.

  1. Availability is only the beginning.A service must be usable at every step.

Author’s synthesis and interpretation

01 / The finding

What the review examined

Mayers and colleagues’ February 2026 overview in BMC Health Services Research synthesized nine systematic reviews of pandemic-era healthcare access among refugee, immigrant, and migrant populations. Searches ended in July 2024. The included reviews were published in 2021–2024 and contained 14–256 primary studies each.[1]

The authors identified both digital exclusion and useful telehealth adaptations, alongside financial, legal, communication, and trust-related barriers. This was a narrative synthesis, not a pooled estimate of intervention effectiveness.[1]

02 / The open question

Where does access break down?

Author’s interpretation

A useful way to evaluate access is to examine what happens between steps. An outreach message must reach someone; its meaning must be understood; a booking must be completed; and the encounter must be usable. Counting only the first or final step hides where participation breaks down.

Consider two services with identical appointment capacity. One requires an English-language account and repeated identity verification. The other offers assisted booking and a telephone route. Comparing appointment availability alone would miss their operational differences. This is an illustrative comparison, not a result from the reviewed studies.

What to examine

Compare outreach channels and ask how participants first heard about the service.

03 / The implication

What should we test next?

The following are proposed research questions, rather than demonstrated effects:

  1. Does assisted booking improve completion? Compare an optional navigator-supported route with usual booking. Define the outcome as a completed appointment, and record why people choose each route.
  2. Where does the process lose participants? Examine invitation, attempted booking, confirmed booking, and attendance separately. At each stage, report how many people completed it out of how many attempted it. Distinguish cancellations from unsuccessful contact.
  3. Who benefits from an alternative route? With appropriate consent, examine differences by preferred language and access needs. Include people who never use the portal, so the evaluation does not begin by excluding those facing the greatest difficulty.

Any local study would need partner involvement, appropriate ethics review, a plan to minimize data collection, and measures chosen with community members. A small feasibility study could establish whether the process is usable before making claims about health outcomes.

What this evidence cannot establish

The review’s English-language restriction, mixed populations, variable quality, and possible overlap of primary studies limit interpretation. Its counts are not counts of unique participants or a refugee-specific effect estimate. Publication in 2026 does not make its underlying evidence contemporary to 2026. It cannot establish the benefit of a particular local navigation program.[1]

The practical next step is a testable service-design question: which change helps people complete a step they previously could not? Answering that carefully would be more informative than treating a new digital platform as proof of improved access.

Reference & editorial note
